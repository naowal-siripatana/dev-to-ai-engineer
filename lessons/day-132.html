DAY 132 · LEVEL 12: LLMOPS · TEACH-BACK

Teach-back: what you would check at 2 a.m.

30 minhands-onfree
Today you will be able to explain in 5 sentences what to check first when an AI system is slow at night, and why.

1 · Warm-up: write down what you know 5 min

Close your notes. It is 2 a.m. and your phone says: “the assistant is slow.” You are half asleep. Write the first three things you would check, in order, and the one thing you would not do.

Do not look anything up. Your guess is the most useful line you will write today.

2 · Explain it 10 min

Your task: explain what you would check at 2 a.m., and why in that order, in 5 sentences or a 60-second voice note.

  • Who you are talking to: a colleague who will be on call tonight, and who has never seen the system.
  • Use these words: request id, median, error, baseline, runbook.
  • Rules: no notes, and use at least one number from your own table.

Write it or record it now. A rough first try is exactly right.

Stuck? Sentence starters

Use these if you freeze:

1. The first thing I check is ... , because it tells me whether ... .
2. Then I look at ... : the number of ..., the ... and the ... .
3. To follow one failing request, I take its ... and search ... .
4. I know what is normal because ... (for example the median is ... ms).
5. I do not ... at 2 a.m. without ... , and I write down ... .

3 · Check it against the key points 8 min

Read the six key points and tick each one that your explanation said clearly. Be strict.

  1. Start with the symptom: is it up, is anything failing, is it slow? The cheapest check that tells you the most comes first.
  2. Know what normal looks like: a baseline (median, error rate, refusal rate). A number means nothing without it.
  3. Look at the middle and the edge: the median, the 95th percentile and the worst case. An average hides a cold start.
  4. Take one bad request: its id, then every log that holds it. One request tells you more than a thousand averages.
  5. Ask what changed: the last deploy, ingest or configuration.
  6. Do little, and write it down: do not change several things at 2 a.m. without a note. The aim is to restore service, and to leave the cause for the morning.
A model answer in 5 sentences

First I check whether the system is up and whether anything is failing, because those two checks are cheap and tell me whether users are hurt. Then I look at the median, the 95th percentile and the worst latency, and I compare them with what is normal: for us the median is about 8 milliseconds, and a very slow first request after a restart is expected. To understand a failure I take the id of one failing request and search for it in the gateway log, the Python log and the audit log, because one request tells me where the time or the error was. I also look at the refusal rate, because a jump from 15 percent to 50 percent would point to the data or the search and not to the users. I change as little as possible at night, and I write down what I saw and did, so that the cause can be found in the morning.

Count your ticks. 6: very clear. 3 to 5: good, you found your gaps. 0 to 2: also fine, the next step fills them in.

4 · Look at the evidence 5 min

Do not trust the argument. This is a rehearsal on 20 real requests through the gateway to the Python service (a stand-in writer, no real model), stored in the metrics table. It is not production traffic, but the queries and the habit are the same:

THE 2 A.M. LIST (cheapest and most informative first)
1  Is it up?                 health endpoint of the gateway and of Python           GET /health  ->  {"status": "ok"}
2  Is anything failing?      errors in the last 15 minutes                          count of rows with outcome = 'error'
3  Is it slow?               median and p95 latency, and the worst one              median 8 ms, p95 10 ms (without the first request), max 3584 ms (the first, cold, request)
4  Is it expensive?          tokens and cost in the last hour                       3609 in / 275 out, 0.004984 USD (example prices)
5  Did behaviour change?     the refusal rate                                       15% of questions refused (a sudden jump = the data or the search broke)
6  Take ONE bad request      its id, then the logs of every service                 grep bad-role gateway.log python.log audit.jsonl
7  What changed lately?      the last deploy, the last ingest, the last config      git log, the audit log
8  Write it down             what you saw, what you did, what you did not do        a note for the morning
-- 2. failing now
SELECT request_id, status_code FROM requests WHERE (status_code >= 400 OR outcome = 'error') AND time > now() - interval '15 minutes';
   ->  bad-role   422

-- 3. slow
SELECT percentile_cont(0.5) WITHIN GROUP (ORDER BY latency_ms), max(latency_ms) FROM requests;
   ->  median 8 ms, max 3584 ms        (the request with the id demo-123, the first after the start)

-- 5. behaviour
SELECT round(100.0 * count(*) FILTER (WHERE outcome = 'refused') / count(*), 1) FROM requests WHERE route = '/api/ask';
   ->  15.0
what is 'normal' here (from the 20 requests)       so an alert would mean
median latency 8 ms, p95 10 ms                          a median of 500 ms would be a real change
first request after a start: about 3.6 s                a slow request right after a restart is expected, not an incident
refused: 15% (3 of 20)                                  50% refused would point to the data or the search, not to the users
error: 1 in 20 (a 422 from a bad role)                  one 4xx is a user mistake; a run of 5xx is an incident

Check your explanation against it. Did you say what is normal, and not only what to look at? Notice the last row of the table: a 4xx is a user’s mistake; a run of 5xx is an incident. An explanation that says “I would look at the logs” is too vague; one that says “I would check up, errors and latency against a baseline, then follow one request by its id” is right.

5 · Fix the biggest gap 3 min

Look at the key points you did not tick. Pick the one that matters most and write a better sentence for it, using a number from the table. Then write your own eight-line runbook for your system, and save it next to your earlier teach-backs.
