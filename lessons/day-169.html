1 · Self-test 25 min

Close your notes. Answer each question in your own words, and write a confidence number next to it: 1 = guessing, 2 = fairly sure, 3 = certain. Then open the answers and mark each one right, partly right or wrong.

  1. In what order does a README answer a stranger’s questions, and what goes near the top? (Day 163)
  2. What does the diagram check compare, and why does it help? (Day 163)
  3. What are the beats of a three-minute demo, and why only things you have run? (Day 165)
  4. What two things must a portfolio story include? (Day 165)
  5. Why clone into a new folder with a new empty database, and what kind of gap does it find? (Day 166)
  6. Name two of the five gaps found by the fresh clone in the course’s own run. (Day 166)
  7. What are the six key points of a pitch? (Day 167)
  8. Which claims in the course project have no real run behind them? (Day 167)
  9. What does the freeze rule say, and what is polish? (Day 168)
Answers
  1. What is this; how does it fit; how do I run it; what can I change; how do I know it works; what should I not trust. The honest part (what is real and what is not) near the top.
  2. The boxes of the diagram against the services of the compose file. It stops the picture from drifting away from the truth.
  3. Who you are and the problem; how it fits; two or three things working; what is not real; what next. Only what you have run: a real result persuades and an unbacked claim damages the rest.
  4. One decision with its evidence, and one thing that broke.
  5. Your working copy and old database hold things the repository does not. It finds gaps between what you know and what you wrote: missing setup, silent skips, examples that fail by default, invisible features, leftover templates.
  6. Any two of: a new database without the pgvector extension; nine tests skipped with no explanation; a template README and a tab title of “chat-ui”; a hint that fails for the default role; a page that never showed sources.
  7. The problem and who has it; what you built; proof; a decision; what is not real; what next.
  8. One command starting everything (images never built), the pipeline on every push (never run on GitHub) and the hosted model (never called).
  9. From the freeze until the workshop: no new features, only fixes to bugs, README and demo. Polish is the small things that make a stranger trust the rest, not new code.

Read your numbers. If you were sure (3) and wrong, that is the most valuable thing you found. Circle those.

2 · Offers and interviews 20 min

You have been sending three to five applications a week for four weeks. Fill in the real numbers, and read what they say. Then prepare for the questions that will come. For each, the answer is not a speech: it is the evidence behind it.

applications and answers this week (use the log)

what the answers say: which kind of posting replies, which of my sentences got a reply

the ONE change for next week: ...
six questions you will be asked, and the evidence behind each answer
1. "Tell me about a project."              the portfolio story of Day 165 (problem, build, a decision, a thing that broke, what is not real, next)
2. "How do you know it works?"             tests (numbers), the golden questions (18), a fresh clone (5 gaps found), what you did NOT run
3. "What would you do if it gave a wrong answer?"   grounding and citations, the refusal rule, the audit log, a vote tied to a request id, a new golden question
4. "Hosted model or a local one?"           your own comparison (small sample, one run): size, memory, speed, cost per question, what quality you saw
5. "What went wrong?"                      a real bug you found and how (the new database without the extension; example prices charged to a local model)
6. "What would you change with more time?" a real login instead of a role header; more golden questions; a Docker run and a pipeline run on a real machine

Say each answer aloud once, in 30 seconds, with the evidence. The one you cannot say is the one to work on.
