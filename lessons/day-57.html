1 · Self-test 25 min

Close your notes. This fortnight was about words again, so answer each question in your own words. Write a confidence number next to each: 1 = guessing, 2 = fairly sure, 3 = certain. Then open the answers and mark each one right, partly right or wrong.

  1. What is a timeout, and why is a ten-minute default a problem in a chat? (Day 51)
  2. Which failures do you retry and which do not, and why? (Day 51)
  3. What are backoff and jitter, and what does Retry-After add? (Day 51)
  4. Why can a stream be retried only before its first piece? (Day 51, Day 46)
  5. What is the difference between a wish and a rule in a prompt? What is few-shot? (Day 53)
  6. Why must the examples in a prompt differ from your test questions? (Day 53)
  7. What does an ablation tell you, and why do you change one thing at a time? (Day 54)
  8. Name the parts of the context of one call, and say which one grows the most. (Day 55)
Answers
  1. The longest you will wait for a call. Without a short one a stuck call can hold the user for ten minutes (the SDK default is 600 seconds), so you set your own, for example 30 seconds.
  2. Retry what may go away: timeouts, lost connections, 429 and server errors. Do not retry what will fail the same way: a wrong key (401) or a bad request (400).
  3. Backoff: wait longer after each failure (for example 0.5 s, 1 s, 2 s, up to a ceiling). Jitter: a random part, so many clients do not retry at the same moment. Retry-After lets the provider say how long to wait.
  4. After a piece has gone to the user, a retry would repeat or mix the text. Before the first piece nothing has been sent, so a retry is safe.
  5. A wish cannot be checked (“be helpful”), a rule can (“at most two short sentences”). Few-shot means giving a few examples of input and output in the prompt, to show the format and the behaviour.
  6. If a test question is in the prompt, a good answer may be memory, so the test no longer shows how the prompt works on new questions. It is the same mistake as tuning on the test set.
  7. You remove one part (a rule, the examples) and measure again, to see which parts matter. With one change at a time, you know which change moved the score.
  8. The system prompt (rules and examples), the retrieved sources, the conversation history and the question. The history grows the most: with 51 turns it was 78 percent of the call.

Read your numbers. If you were sure (3) and wrong, that is the most valuable thing you found. Circle those.
