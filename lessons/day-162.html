1 · Self-test 25 min

A note on this module. The serving lessons were run on one Apple-silicon laptop with a small server from the mlx-lm library and Qwen2.5 models. Ollama and vLLM were not run; what the lessons say about them comes from their documentation. Your own runs are the ones that count, and section 3 asks you to write down what you ran.

Close your notes. Answer each question in your own words, and write a confidence number next to it: 1 = guessing, 2 = fairly sure, 3 = certain. Then open the answers and mark each one right, partly right or wrong.

  1. What does open-weight mean, and what does it not mean? (Day 156)
  2. How much memory do the weights of a 13 billion model need in 16 bits and in 4 bits? Show the calculation. (Day 156, 160)
  3. What does quantization trade? How do you find out whether the trade is acceptable? (Day 156, 160)
  4. What is an OpenAI-compatible server, and what three things change when you switch? (Day 158)
  5. Why is the first call slow, and why did a small model invent a meaning for E104? (Day 158)
  6. Where is the provider chosen in your code, and why only there? (Day 159)
  7. What did the cost column show for a local model before the fix, and what does a cost of 0 not mean? (Day 159, 161)
  8. Why does the comparison keep retrieval and prompt the same, and what is wrong with concluding from 5 questions and one run? (Day 161)
  9. Why must a model server stay behind a reverse proxy? (Day 161)
Answers
  1. The weights can be downloaded. It does not mean open source: data and code may be closed and the license may limit use.
  2. Parameters times bits divided by 8: 13 billion x 16 / 8 = 26 GB; in 4 bits about 6.5 GB, or about 7 GB at 4.5 bits with the extras. Plus room for the context and the program.
  3. Precision for size and speed. You find out by testing on your own questions, because how much worse it gets depends on the model and the task.
  4. A server that copies the request shape of a popular chat API. The address (base_url), the model name and sometimes the key change.
  5. The model loads into memory on the first call (warm it up). The model had no sources and no knowledge of your product, so it guessed.
  6. In one function that takes the settings and returns the client, so that the rest of the code never asks which provider it is.
  7. It charged the example API price to a model with no per-token fee. A cost of 0 per token does not mean free: the machine, its electricity and your time cost something.
  8. So any difference comes from the model. The sample is small and one run each: a question or two flipping changes the order, so it is a hint, not a verdict.
  9. The server has only basic security. The proxy is the single public door that ends HTTPS, checks who calls and limits the rate.

Read your numbers. If you were sure (3) and wrong, that is the most valuable thing you found. Circle those.
