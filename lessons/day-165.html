DAY 165 · LEVEL 13: SHOW YOUR WORK

Demo script and portfolio story

60-70 minhands-onfree
Today you will be able to write a three-minute demo script of things you have really run, and a 150-word portfolio story.

1 · Warm-up 3 min

Answer from memory, then open the answers.

  1. In what order does a README answer a stranger’s questions?
  2. What does the diagram check compare?
Answers
  1. What is this; how does it fit; how do I run it; what can I change; how do I know it works; what should I not trust.
  2. The boxes of the diagram against the services of the compose file.

2 · Hook 2 min

You get three minutes in front of a hiring manager, or a room at the workshop. You have a whole system. What do you show, in what order, and what do you leave out?

Guess first, then open

Show one path through the system that a stranger can follow, not every feature. Start with the problem in one sentence, show three or four things working, say plainly what is not real, and end with what you would do next. What you leave out is most of what you built: that is the skill.

3 · The idea, in three small pieces 8 min

Piece 1: a demo is a story with beats

A three-minute demo has about six beats: who you are and the problem (20 seconds), how it fits (30), two or three things working (about 90), what is not real (30), and what next (20). Every beat shows or says one thing. If a step can fail on stage, rehearse it, and have a fallback (a recording or screenshots).

Piece 2: only show what you have run

The most persuasive thing in a demo is a real result, and the most damaging is a claim you cannot back up. So the script contains only things you have run on this machine, and it says what is a stand-in and what was not run. A listener who hears you say “this part is a stand-in” believes the rest.

Piece 3: the portfolio story is the same story in writing

The same six parts, written in about 150 words, are your answer to “tell me about a project” in an interview, and the text under your project on a CV or LinkedIn. It must include one decision with its evidence and one thing that broke: those two show how you think.

If you know another typed language (C# as the example): the demo script is a test plan for a human: Arrange (start it), Act (three questions), Assert (what you say each result proves). Rehearsing it is running the test twice.
Capstone: the script you write today is the demo you record on Saturday and run at the workshop.

[1:15]  Now a question the pages cannot answer: where can I buy a coffee?
        It says it could not find that in the knowledge base. A refusal is a correct answer, and it is counted as one.
[1:35]  Roles matter. As a customer I ask what error E104 means, and I am refused: error records are for staff.
        (switch to staff, ask again) Now it answers, and cites the error page.
[2:00]  And I can see how it is doing: the dashboard shows questions, success rate, time and an estimated cost.
        These numbers come from a rehearsal of a few dozen questions, not from real users.
[2:20]  Now the honest part. The writer here is a stand-in, not a model, so everything runs and is tested with no key.
        I also ran it once with a small model on my own machine and compared it on my questions, and I wrote down how small that test was.
        I did not run the Docker images or the pipeline on GitHub myself, and the README says so.
[2:50]  What I would do next: real users, a real login instead of a role in a header, and a bigger set of test questions.
        The code, the diagram and the tests are in the repository. Thank you.

Look at what the script does not do: it does not show the tests, the CI file, the Docker files or the code. They are in the repository, and the README points to them. It does say, near the end, what was a stand-in and what was never run.

The same story, as a portfolio paragraph. The six parts first:

You should see
PORTFOLIO STORY (about 150 words, spoken or written)
1. The problem          one sentence a non-technical person understands
2. What I built         the parts, and one number or fact that proves it runs
3. A decision           one choice I made, the alternative, and the evidence that decided it
4. A thing that broke   what went wrong, how I found it, what I changed
5. What is not real     what is a stand-in, never run or too small to conclude from
6. What next            one concrete step

And an example of about 137 words (the numbers in it are the real ones from this course’s runs; write yours):

A support team answers the same questions all day, and a chatbot that invents answers is risky.
I built an assistant of four parts (a React page, a .NET gateway, a Python service, PostgreSQL with vector search) that answers only from its own pages, cites them, and refuses when they do not hold the answer.
One decision: I compared three writers on my 18 test questions; the stand-in passed 11, and a small local model passed between 5 and 17 depending on its size and precision, so I wrote down that the sample was small.
Something broke: a fresh clone failed because a new database had no vector extension. The README test caught it, and the store now creates it.
What is not real: the default writer is a stand-in, and I never ran the Docker images or the GitHub pipeline myself.
Next: a real login and more test questions.

5 · Try it 30 min

Work in docs/ of your capstone repository, on a branch: git switch -c demo.

Step 1 Copy and run

Run every beat of your demo for real, on a fresh start of your system, and write down what each one shows (a table like the one in the worked example). If a beat fails, fix it or cut it. Do not write a script for a demo you have not run.

Step 2 Change one thing

Write the script in the six beats with the plan times. Count the spoken words. Predict: will it fit in three minutes at about 130 words a minute? Then read it aloud with a timer and see.

Step 3 Break it on purpose

Say what can go wrong on stage: the server is not running, the first question is slow because a model is loading, the network is down. For each, write the fallback in one line (a warm-up before you start, a recorded clip, a screenshot). Now stop your database and run beat 1: what does the page show? Is that a failure you can explain in one sentence?

Step 4 Your turn

Write your portfolio story in about 150 words with the six parts, including one decision with its evidence and one thing that broke. Read it aloud once. Commit: git add docs/demo-script.md docs/portfolio-story.md and git commit -m "Add a demo script and a portfolio story".

6 · Stuck? anytime

  • The script is too long: cut features, not the honest part. Three working things are enough.
  • You cannot think of a decision with evidence: look in your own work for a comparison you ran (two chunk sizes, two prompts, two models, with and without a cache) and the number that decided it.
  • You cannot think of something that broke: open your error log. The best one is a bug that a test or a fresh clone found.
  • You are afraid of saying what is not real: it is the opposite of a weakness. Say it in one calm sentence, then move on.
Hint: Step 2

Count the words of the spoken lines only (not the brackets). About 390 words is three minutes at 130 a minute; reading aloud with real pauses is usually slower than you expect.

Solution: Step 3

The page shows an error message from the gateway (it cannot reach the service or the database), not a blank screen. On stage, say it in one sentence (“the database is down, so it cannot look anything up; it says so instead of guessing”) and use your fallback clip. Rehearse it once so that it is a feature, not a surprise.

7 · Check yourself 5 min

  1. What are the beats of a three-minute demo?
  2. Why does the script contain only things you have run?
  3. What two things must a portfolio story include, and why?
Answers
  1. Who you are and the problem; how it fits; two or three things working; what is not real; what next.
  2. A real result persuades, and a claim you cannot back up damages everything else. Saying what is a stand-in makes the rest believable.
  3. One decision with its evidence, and one thing that broke: they show how you think and how you fix things.
