1 · Self-test 25 min

Close your notes. This test goes across the three months. Answer each question in your own words, and write a confidence number next to it: 1 = guessing, 2 = fairly sure, 3 = certain. Then open the answers and mark each one right, partly right or wrong.

  1. What is a token, and why does it matter for cost and for the context window? (Module 3)
  2. What does an embedding capture, and what does it miss? (Module 3, Day 74)
  3. What does retrieval-augmented generation add to a plain model call? (Module 6)
  4. Why is a chunk size a trade-off, and how did you choose yours? (Day 68)
  5. Why does a high similarity not prove that the answer is in the page? (Day 75)
  6. Where must a permission filter be applied, and why not in the prompt? (Day 79)
  7. Who runs the function in a tool call, and what does the tool_use_id link? (Day 81)
  8. What does a good tool error contain, and why is the permission checked first? (Day 86)
  9. What does MCP add, and why does it not replace function calling? (Day 88)
  10. Name two ways your assistant says “I do not know” and one way it could still be wrong. (Module 6)
Answers
  1. A token is a piece of text (a word or part of a word) that the model reads and writes. Cost and the size of the context window are counted in tokens.
  2. It captures meaning: texts about the same thing are close. It can miss exact words and codes, such as E104.
  3. The model answers from sources that you retrieved from your own data, instead of from its memory, and can cite them.
  4. Small chunks look alike and lose context; big chunks blur topics and cost tokens. The answer is a measured middle on your own questions, not a rule.
  5. The score says something similar exists, not that the answer is in it. The right page may not have been retrieved.
  6. In the query, before ranking, for every search path. A prompt is a request, and the model already has the text.
  7. Your code runs it. The id links a tool_result to the tool_use it answers.
  8. A stable code, a plain message and a hint for the next step. The permission comes first so that a refused caller learns nothing about the data.
  9. It standardises how a host finds and runs tools, so a tool is written once. The model still asks for a tool with function calling; MCP sits between the host and the tools.
  10. It refuses when the best similarity is low, and when the writer finds no answer or the citations are invalid. It can still give a wrong answer with a real citation, as the price question did.

Read your numbers. If you were sure (3) and wrong, that is the most valuable thing you found. Circle those.
