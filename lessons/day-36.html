1 · Self-test 25 min

Close your notes. Answer each question in your own words, and write a confidence number next to it: 1 = guessing, 2 = fairly sure, 3 = certain. Then open the answers and mark each one right, partly right or wrong.

  1. What is a token, and why is it not the same as a word or a character? (Day 32)
  2. What shares the context window in one call, and what happens if the prompt fills all of it? (Day 32)
  3. The same Thai sentence was 6 tokens with one encoding and 18 with another. What do you learn from that? (Day 32)
  4. Why does pasting the whole knowledge base into every prompt stop working at real size? Give two reasons. (Day 33)
  5. Why is the price kept in one named constant, and why must you read your provider’s pricing page? (Day 33)
  6. Explain attention in two sentences, with your own analogy. (Day 34)
  7. Your splitter must keep two promises. Which, and how do you test them? (Day 35)
  8. A language model answers one question over and over. Which, and how does it write a sentence? (Day 30)
Answers
  1. A token is a piece of text that a tokenizer learned is common: a word, part of a word, a space plus a word, a number, a symbol. So a word can be one token or several, and the count depends on the text and on the model.
  2. The prompt (question, instructions, documents) and the answer share it. If the prompt fills the window, there is no room for an answer, and the call fails or the text is cut.
  3. Each model family has its own tokenizer, so a count only makes sense for the model it was made for. Count with a tokenizer that matches your model.
  4. It does not fit in the context window, and it costs the full price of all the tokens for every question (and makes each call slower). Irrelevant text can also make answers worse.
  5. Prices change and differ by provider, so one constant is one place to update. The lesson used an example price, not a real one.
  6. For example: “When a model reads a word, attention lets it look at all the other words and listen mostly to the ones that matter for its question, like listening to the helpful voices at a noisy party.”
  7. Every chunk fits the budget (count the tokens of each piece), and no text is lost (join the pieces and compare with the original).
  8. “Which token comes next?” It writes by repeating: score every possible next token, pick one, add it, and predict again.

Read your numbers. If you were sure (3) and wrong, that is the most valuable thing you found this week. Circle those.
