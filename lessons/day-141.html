1 · Self-test 25 min

Close your notes. Answer each question in your own words, and write a confidence number next to it: 1 = guessing, 2 = fairly sure, 3 = certain. Then open the answers and mark each one right, partly right or wrong.

  1. What does a timeout cost you? What does a fallback need to say about itself? (Day 135)
  2. What goes into a safe cache key, and what limit must you write down? (Day 135, 138)
  3. Why must a vote carry a request id? What do you do with the down-votes? (Day 137)
  4. What are an SLI, an SLO and an error budget? Give your own definition of a good request. (Day 137, 139)
  5. Why does a 4xx not count against the SLI? (Day 137)
  6. What did the SLI find in your numbers, and how was it fixed? (Day 137)
  7. Which of the five numbers hides the most, and what does it hide? (Day 133, 140)
  8. Why only staff on the statistics endpoint, and what is the gap in that rule? (Day 140)
  9. What is estimated, example or stand-in in your numbers? (Day 130, 140)
Answers
  1. A slow failure costs the user the whole timeout before the second try. A fallback must say which model answered, so that the metrics show how often and at what quality.
  2. The normalised question, the role and anything else that changes the answer (top_k). The limit: an answer can be as old as the time to live, and a change to the pages is not seen until it expires or the cache is cleared.
  3. So that the vote can be investigated (question, sources, tokens, logs). Read them, turn bad answers into new golden questions, fix, and check that the score table improves and nothing else gets worse.
  4. An SLI measures what the user feels; an SLO is the target for it; the error budget is what the target leaves (100% minus the objective). A good request here: not a 5xx, and under 2 seconds.
  5. It is the caller’s mistake, not a failure of your service; counting it would punish you for what you cannot fix.
  6. One cold start of 3.6 s among 20 requests made the SLI 95%, below the 99% objective. A warm-up at start fixed it: the first request went from 3447 ms and 596 ms to 42 and 34 ms.
  7. The average time hides slow requests; the cost hides estimated tokens and example prices; the success rate counts a refusal as a success.
  8. The statistics describe how the system is used. The gap: the role is a demo header that a caller can forge, so a customer could ask as staff until there is real authentication.
  9. The tokens are estimates (tiktoken), the prices are examples, the writer is a stand-in, and the 24 requests are a rehearsal, not production.

Read your numbers. If you were sure (3) and wrong, that is the most valuable thing you found. Circle those.

2 · Module 12 retrospective 20 min

Module 12 asked for one thing: a dashboard backed by logs and traces. You have request ids across two services, a metrics table, a fallback, a cache, votes, an SLO and a dashboard with a screenshot. Answer these in your notebook, two lines each:

Module 12 retrospective (2 lines each, with an example)
1. Which number on my dashboard do I trust most, and which least? Why?
2. What did my numbers find that I would not have found by looking at the code? (a cold start? a wrong answer a user voted down?)
3. What can still go wrong at 2 a.m. that my dashboard would NOT show?
4. What is estimated, or an example, or a stand-in, in my numbers? Is it written next to them?
5. If a stranger read my README, could they tell a rehearsal from production?

Look at question 4 and question 5. The most useful sentence in a README for an operations story is one that tells the reader what is not real. People trust a number more when its limits are written next to it.

3 · Job applications: three to five a week 30 min

From now on, the plan asks for three to five applications a week. It is a routine: the same days, the same 45 minutes, and a log. It is not a promise of an answer, and it is not a measure of your worth. It gives you feedback from the market while you keep learning.

THREE TO FIVE A WEEK: a routine, not a mood
 Mon, Wed, Fri      one application each (45 minutes: Day 99), plus Tue and Thu: one message to a person (a developer, a recruiter, a teacher) about their work
 each one            a different posting; the project that fits goes first in the CV; five honest sentences; send; log with a follow-up date
 after two weeks     look at the log: which kind of posting answers? Which of my sentences got a reply? Change ONE thing.
 what to show        the README of the repository, the dashboard screenshot (with its caption), the ADR, the golden table, the security table
 what to say         what you built, what is a stand-in, and what you would do next. Never a claim you cannot show.

Now do two things. First, write the five sentences that you will adapt for every application: who you are, what you built, what is a stand-in, what you measured, what you would do next. Second, open your application log, and look for a pattern: which postings answered, and which of your sentences got a reply?
