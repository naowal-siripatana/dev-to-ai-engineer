1 · Self-test 25 min

Close your notes. Answer each question in your own words, and write a confidence number next to it: 1 = guessing, 2 = fairly sure, 3 = certain. Then open the answers and mark each one right, partly right or wrong.

  1. What is an embedding, and why can a computer find “too hot” for the question “getting very warm”? (Day 37)
  2. Why must the question and the documents use the same embedding model? (Day 37)
  3. A question about coffee still got a closest document. What does that teach you, and what is the fix? (Day 37, Day 42)
  4. A model call is stateless. What does that mean for a conversation? (Day 39)
  5. Where do the system prompt, the user messages and the model’s earlier answers go in a request? (Day 39)
  6. The best match changed when you added documents, and again when you embedded the body only. What does that teach you? (Day 40)
  7. Give one example where keywords beat embeddings and one where embeddings beat keywords. (Day 41)
  8. What are the two guards that let your prototype say “I could not find that”? (Day 42)
Answers
  1. An embedding is a fixed-length list of numbers that stands for the meaning of a text. Texts with similar meaning get similar numbers, so “too hot” ends up close to “getting very warm” without a shared word.
  2. Vectors from different models live in different spaces. They may not even have the same length, and comparing them means nothing.
  3. There is always a nearest document, even for an unrelated question. Add a minimum score on the best match, and an instruction in the prompt to say a fixed sentence when the sources do not contain the answer.
  4. The model keeps nothing between calls. Your code keeps the history and sends it all again on every call, so a long chat costs more with every turn.
  5. The system prompt goes in the system field (other providers use a message with role system). The user messages and the model’s earlier answers go in the list of messages, with roles user and assistant.
  6. You can only find what is in the index, and what you embed (title plus body, or body only) changes what you find. Test with real questions.
  7. Keywords beat embeddings for exact strings, such as the error code E105 (embeddings tied it with E101). Embeddings beat keywords for paraphrases, such as “getting very warm” and “too hot”.
  8. A minimum score on the best match (skip the model if it is too low), and an instruction in the prompt to say a fixed sentence when the sources do not contain the answer.

Read your numbers. If you were sure (3) and wrong, that is the most valuable thing you found. Circle those.
