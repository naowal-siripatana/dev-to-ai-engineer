DAY 120 · LEVEL 10: DESIGN · REVIEW

Self-test: design trade-offs

1.5 hhands-onfree
Today you will be able to explain framing, the ladder of approaches and your ADR in your own words, and plan week 18.

1 · Self-test 25 min

Close your notes. Answer each question in your own words, and write a confidence number next to it: 1 = guessing, 2 = fairly sure, 3 = certain. Then open the answers and mark each one right, partly right or wrong.

  1. What four things belong in the framing of a problem? (Day 114)
  2. What makes a requirement testable? Rewrite “it should be fast”. (Day 114)
  3. Why write “where you are today” next to the success criterion? (Day 114)
  4. Name the five rungs of the ladder from the simplest. (Day 116)
  5. Why did a dictionary beat a vector search for the error code? (Day 116)
  6. Why might RAG be the wrong choice for a knowledge base of 862 tokens? (Day 116)
  7. Why does the order of the questions in your framework matter? (Day 117)
  8. Give three reasons the project uses RAG and not fine-tuning, with a number for each. (Day 118)
  9. What does a good ADR contain that many ADRs leave out? (Day 119)
Answers
  1. Who has the problem, the job, the pain today, and the non-goals.
  2. It says one thing and comes with a check (a yes or no, or a number, and the script). “Retrieval p95 under 50 ms (model call not included)” is testable.
  3. The gap between the two is the plan, and it keeps the criterion honest: do not lower it to match the numbers.
  4. A rule or lookup, one model call, RAG, an agent, a person.
  5. A rule is exact, instant (about 0.1 microseconds against 6.5 ms) and always right, while the vector search alone did not have the E104 page in its top 3.
  6. All 20 pages are 862 tokens: they fit in a prompt, so you could send every page. RAG is then justified by growth, per-user filtering and citations, which must be written down.
  7. The first question that fits decides, so the order says which concern wins: an exact rule beats everything, and a costly mistake beats a clever loop. Tests of the order keep it that way.
  8. Updates: changing one page took 13 ms and the next answer used it. Permissions: 0 of 7 staff-only pages seen in 18 questions as a customer. Sources: every answer cites a chunk. (Also: retrieval and writing can be scored apart.)
  9. The alternative that would win in some conditions, what was not tried, and a “revisit when” for each decision.

Read your numbers. If you were sure (3) and wrong, that is the most valuable thing you found. Circle those.

2 · Your decision log 15 min

An ADR records a big decision. Smaller ones need a place too. Keep a one-line log of every decision you take, with its evidence, what you did not try, and when you would revisit it. After a month it tells you which decisions you can no longer explain:

decision log (one line per decision you take)
date | decision | the evidence (a run, a number) | what I did NOT try | revisit when
...  | RAG, not fine-tuning | update 13 ms; 0 staff pages seen | fine-tuning | the data stops changing and a style is needed

4 · Run it from cold 15 min

Stop everything, then start again from your README and AGENTS.md only: the database, ingest.py, the tests, run_golden.py. Then open docs/problem.md and docs/adr/0001-...md and check that every number in them is still true when you run the scripts again. A number that no longer matches means the document is out of date: fix it, or write a new ADR.

Group this week’s entries into 3 themes, pick the top one, and fix it with one tiny experiment (5 minutes). Then open My Learning and look for Days that are not ticked (Day 113 to Day 119): finish in order, steps before games.

6 · Job applications 15 min

Keep the claims true: say that the answer quality was tested with a stand-in writer, if that is still the case.
