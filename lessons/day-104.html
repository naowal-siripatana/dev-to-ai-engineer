Teach-back: LLM-as-judge, pros and cons

30 minhands-onfree
Today you will be able to explain in 5 sentences what an LLM-as-judge is good for, what it costs, and when to keep exact checks.

1 · Warm-up: write down what you know 5 min

Close your notes. A colleague says: “Why write all these scripts? Just ask a model whether the answer is good.” Write what you would answer in two lines. Then write one thing a judge model might do better than your script, and one way it could go wrong.

Do not look anything up. Your guess is the most useful line you will write today.

2 · Explain it 10 min

Your task: explain LLM-as-judge, with its pros and cons, in 5 sentences or a 60-second voice note.

  • Who you are talking to: the colleague above, who wants to use a model for everything.
  • Use these words: judge, rubric, exact check, bias, calibrate.
  • Rules: no notes, and be fair: say what a judge is good for, and what it costs.

Write it or record it now. A rough first try is exactly right.

Stuck? Sentence starters

Use these if you freeze:

1. An LLM-as-judge is ... : a model that is asked to ... another answer.
2. It helps when ..., because exact checks cannot ... .
3. Its risks are ... (for example ...), so ... .
4. I would still keep exact checks for ..., because they are ... .
5. Before I trust a judge, I ... : I compare it with ... on ... answers.

3 · Check it against the key points 8 min

Read the six key points and tick each one that your explanation said clearly. Be strict.

  1. What it is: a model that is given the question, the sources and the answer, and a short rubric, and returns a verdict (for example faithful and complete, yes or no, with a reason).
  2. Where it helps: open-ended answers, where the same fact can be worded many ways, so an exact phrase check is too strict, and where completeness and tone matter.
  3. Where it hurts: it costs a model call per answer, it can vary between runs, and it can be wrong.
  4. Biases: judges tend to like longer answers, answers that sound confident, and answers in the style of their own model, and can be swayed by the order in which answers are shown.
  5. Keep exact checks where they work: retrieval (is the page found), refusals, formats. They are free, instant and repeatable.
  6. Calibrate it: before you trust a judge, compare its verdicts with your own on 10 to 20 answers, with a simple rubric of yes or no questions, and a low randomness setting.
A model answer in 5 sentences

An LLM-as-judge is a model that gets the question, the sources, the answer and a short rubric, and returns a verdict such as faithful and complete, with a reason. It helps for open-ended answers, where an exact phrase check is too strict and cannot see whether the answer is complete. But it costs a model call for every answer, it can give a different verdict from run to run, and it has biases, for example towards longer and more confident answers. So I keep exact checks for retrieval and refusals, where they are free and repeatable, and I use a judge only for what they cannot see. Before I trust a judge, I compare it with my own verdicts on a few answers, and I keep a rubric of simple yes or no questions.

Count your ticks. 6: very clear. 3 to 5: good, you found your gaps. 0 to 2: also fine, the next step fills them in.

A rubric a judge could use (shown as text, not run), and a summary to check your answer against:

You are checking an answer from a support assistant.
Question: {question}
Sources the assistant was given: {sources}
Answer: {answer}

Reply with JSON only: {"faithful": true or false, "complete": true or false, "reason": "one short sentence"}
- faithful: every claim in the answer is supported by the sources.
- complete: the answer contains what the user needs to act, taken from the sources.
                     exact checks (scripts)              LLM-as-judge
what it checks       a page id, a phrase, a refusal      meaning: faithful? complete? helpful?
cost and speed       free and instant                    a model call for every answer
repeatable           yes: same input, same score         no: can vary between runs
good for             retrieval, refusals, formats        open-ended answers, tone, completeness
main risks           too strict (a paraphrase fails)     too lenient; biased; wrong; costs money

Did your explanation say why a judge is needed (the gaps above)? Did it say what a judge costs? An explanation that says “a judge model tells you whether the answer is good” claims too much; one that says “a judge covers what exact checks cannot see, and must be checked against humans” is right.
